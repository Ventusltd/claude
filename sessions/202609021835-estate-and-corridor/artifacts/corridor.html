Grid Corridor Engine

A maths kernel, a stress harness, and a routed-corridor service for GridAtlas. It replaces the straight line between a project and a substation with a route a cable could actually take, and it makes every geometric claim the Atlas renders falsifiable by test. The routing gate has since been run: see 00.

00 Verdict — the gate was run, and it failed

Sections 03–07 below were written before the feasibility gate was tested. It has since been run against the 95 published cable circuits, and independently recomputed. Road routing does not earn its complexity. The sections are left standing because the architecture, the stress harness and the phasing remain right; only the routing recommendation is overturned. What replaces it is simpler and measurably better.

What replaces the router

One number. k = 1.245, fitted to 95 published cable circuits across 59 distinct site pairs, bootstrap range 1.22–1.33. It puts 73% of circuits inside 15% of their real built length, needs no graph, no snapping, and cannot fail. Routing managed 20.3% and failed outright on 14 circuits whose endpoints were more than 5 km from any road in the dataset.

Why routing lost

  • It breaks worst where it is needed most. Circuits whose route stayed within 2× the straight line scored 14.2% error — but 14 circuits above 2× scored 199.3%, against 25.7% for the straight line on those same circuits. All urban. Dewar Place to Whitehouse: published 2.48 km, straight 1.83 km, routed 18.67 km.
  • Crossing penalties made it worse, monotonically. Sweeping a rail penalty from 0 to 20 km moved median error from 20.26% to 33.32%. The best penalty is zero. Crossing count correlates with routing error at Spearman −0.12 — no signal. The “avoid railways” weighting in §05 is not supported by evidence at this resolution.
  • Snapping is a real cost, not a rounding term. 41 of 190 endpoints sat more than 1 km from any routable road; the worst was 15.19 km. Remote Scottish and offshore sites cannot be reached by a road graph at all — one validation circuit is a 39 km subsea cable.
The bound on this negative result

Only primary, trunk and motorway roads exist in the estate. No secondary, tertiary or residential roads were available, and the observed failure mode — urban detours where the real link uses minor streets — points directly at that gap. Adding motorways moved median error by nothing (20.26% → 20.26%), so the missing coverage is minor roads, not major ones. A dense OSM network is therefore untested, not disproven. But the bar it must now clear is 8.4%, not 15%, because the constant already passes. That is a much harder target for far more machinery.

Two corrections this forced on the scope

  • The sample is 59, not 95. Parallel circuits duplicate the same geometry between the same two sites. Every confidence statement must use the pair count.
  • Five circuits are geometrically impossible — the straight line exceeds the published built length. The worst publishes 0.33 km against a 24.78 km straight line. That is the name-join defect of §01 surfacing independently in a second dataset: the join has bound a circuit to the wrong substation. It corroborates the 34 colliding keys and raises their priority again.

The finding that sets the priority

Choosing between a spherical and an ellipsoidal earth model moves a 15 km answer by roughly 0.1–0.3%. The route factor moves it by 3–48%. Refining the earth model while quoting a straight line is polishing a rounding error inside a much larger one. The corridor is where the accuracy is.

03 Module A — the maths kernel

Pure functions over coordinates. No fetching, no globals, no knowledge of substations. Everything here is testable without a network and runs identically in Node and Python.

Already present

  • distanceKm, distanceEllipsoidalKm, pathLengthKm
  • initialBearingDeg, destinationPoint
  • distanceToSegmentKm, distanceToLineKm
  • geodesicCircle, circleApproximationError
  • polygonAreaKm2, SpatialIndex

To add

  • snapToGraph(point, graph, maxKm) — nearest routable node with the snap distance returned, never hidden
  • route(a, b, graph, weights) — A* with an admissible great-circle heuristic
  • crossings(path, barriers) — segment-intersection counts by barrier class
  • corridorLength(path) — geodesic polyline length, not planar
  • routeFactor(path, straight) — the ratio, reported not applied
  • simplify(path, toleranceM) — geodesic-aware Douglas-Peucker
Design rule

A snap distance is part of the answer. If a substation is 900 m from the nearest routable road, the route is 900 m short at one end and the card must say so. Silently snapping is how a routing engine starts lying.

04 Module B — the stress harness

Four independent layers. A geometry bug that survives all four is a bug nobody on this estate could have found by reading.

1. Invariants — property-based, thousands of random cases

identity        d(a,a) = 0
symmetry        d(a,b) = d(b,a)                     to 1e-9 relative
triangle        d(a,c) ≤ d(a,b) + d(b,c)
bearing round   destination(a, bearing(a,b), d(a,b)) = b
route ≥ line    routeKm ≥ straightKm                 always, no exceptions
route factor    1.0 ≤ factor ≤ 10.0                  outside this, fail loudly
path length     sum of leg lengths = corridorLength(path)
determinism     same input, same output, across both implementations

2. Adversarial cases — a fixed corpus, not random

  • Coincident and near-coincident points (1 mm, 1 cm, 1 m apart)
  • Antipodal and near-antipodal pairs — where iterative inverse solutions fail to converge
  • The poles, and paths crossing them
  • The antimeridian, in both directions
  • Points either side of the Greenwich meridian and the equator, for sign handling
  • Degenerate graphs: an isolated node, a disconnected component, a zero-length edge
  • A substation with no road within 50 km — the engine must return no route, not a wrong one

3. Oracles — independent, external, authoritative

4. The acceptance test that matters

Self-validating, using data you already publish

NESO publishes cable_km for 95 circuits whose endpoints both have coordinates. Those are real buried cable routes with a known built length. Route between the two substations along the road graph and compare. The straight line is wrong by a median of 34% on this set. A routed engine that does not beat that is not earning its complexity.

Gate: median absolute error against published cable_km must fall below 15%, and the routed estimate must beat the straight line on at least 80% of the 95 circuits. Published as a report, not asserted.

05 Module C — routing, as originally scoped (overturned by 00)

Following roads already solves most of it

A cable route follows made ground because the wayleave, the trenching and the crossings are all cheaper there. Where a road crosses a railway it does so at an existing bridge or culvert — infrastructure that already has the crossing solved. So route along roads delivers most of avoid railways for free, and it is why the measured cable factor is 1.34 rather than 1.0.

Avoidance is the wrong model; cost is the right one

A hard prohibition makes some routes impossible and others absurd — a project on the far side of a main line has no route at all. Every crossing is a consent event with a cost and a duration, so the correct structure is a weighted graph where a crossing is expensive rather than forbidden. Set the weight high enough and avoidance falls out; set it to infinity and you get a broken engine.

weight(edge) = lengthKm
             + RAIL_PENALTY_KM     × railwayCrossings(edge)
             + MOTORWAY_PENALTY_KM × motorwayCrossings(edge)
             + WATER_PENALTY_KM    × watercourseCrossings(edge)

penalties are km-equivalents, published in the product and tunable,
never hardcoded in the cartridge. the card reports the crossing COUNT
as a measurement and the penalty as an assumption, separately.

Defaults to start from, to be calibrated against the 95 cable circuits rather than believed: rail 3.0 km, motorway 2.0 km, watercourse 1.0 km. If calibration says rail should be 8 km, that is a finding about GB consenting, and worth publishing on its own.

193 MB of GeoJSON is not fetchable per session. Two products solve it, and the first is enough for v2:

  • routed-distances.v1 — precomputed offline. For every REPD project, the routed corridor to its nearest N connection points. Roughly 7,680 projects × 5 ≈ 38,400 routes, minutes of CI, a few MB published. The Atlas reads a routed distance the same way it reads a rating. This is v2.
  • corridor-graph.v1 — the graph itself, junction-contracted and spatially tiled, typed-array encoded, fetched by tile so an arbitrary click can be routed in the browser. This is v3, and only if v2 proves the value.

08 Where this could go wrong

  • OSM road coverage is not uniform. Primary and trunk only; a substation reached by an unclassified lane will snap badly. The snap distance makes this visible rather than silent, and adding highway=unclassified is a data decision with a size cost, not a code change.
  • A routed distance will be read as a route. It is a corridor length, not a wayleave, not a consent, not a cost. The card language must carry that as firmly as it already carries “a rating is not what is free on the circuit”.
  • Calibration on 95 circuits is a small sample. Report the confidence interval, and expect it to widen for regions with few cable circuits.
  • Overhead lines must never be road-routed. Their measured factor is 1.13 and they cross open country. Applying the road engine to an OHL question would be a worse answer than the straight line.
  • OSM is ODbL. Unlike BSD-3 on code, the share-alike obligation reaches derived data. A published derived graph needs its licence position settled before it ships.

09 Literature — and what belongs in data-grid-gb

Four sources, verified this session by fetching them rather than by recall. The National Grid document was downloaded and its text extracted directly; the CIGRE and IET references were confirmed against their publishers.

sourcewhat it fixes for route computation
National Grid — Undergrounding high voltage electricity transmission lines: the technical issues Working width 40–65 m for a 400 kV double circuit. Trench about 1.5 m wide and 1.2 m deep, four trenches of three cables. Joint bays every 500–1,000 m, concrete-lined and wider than the trench. Deep tunnelling named as the technique that makes river and railway crossings possible.
IET / Parsons Brinckerhoff (2012) — Electricity Transmission Costing Study Prices trenchless crossings as discrete events and categorises watercourses by span — large river about 150 m, medium about 70 m. This is what lets a crossing penalty be a cited number rather than a guess.
CIGRE TB 770, WG B1.48 — Trenchless Technologies Horizontal directional drilling, microtunnelling, pipe ramming and ploughing for HV cable. Defines which technique suits which obstacle and span — the basis for classifying a crossing rather than merely counting it.
CIGRE TB 889, WG B1.61 (2022) — Installation of Underground HV Cable Systems Supersedes TB 194 (2001). Civil construction and installation practice: the reference for bend radii, pulling tensions and drum section lengths, which constrain what a corridor may geometrically do.
The finding that changes the router

A 400 kV underground circuit needs a 40–65 m working swathe. That is wider than most B-road verges and many A-road corridors. So “follow the road” is necessary but not sufficient — the router needs a corridor width feasibility term, not only a length. A route down a hedgerowed lane is not a route for a 400 kV double circuit, and an engine that returns one is confidently wrong.

Should you write literature into data-grid-gb? Yes — but not as prose

That repository’s discipline is that sources are pinned and products are derived and verified. An essay cannot be verified. A parameter extracted from a cited source can be. So the literature should arrive in the same shape as everything else there:

sources/literature-manifest.json    pinned by SHA-256, exactly like the NESO appendices
derived/civil-parameters.v1.json    the numbers, each carrying its citation

{
  "schema": "data-grid-gb.civil-parameters.v1",
  "not_a_design": "published typical values, not a design for any route",
  "parameters": [
    { "id": "swathe_width_m_400kv_double",
      "value": [40, 65], "unit": "m",
      "source": "national-grid-undergrounding-technical-issues",
      "note": "construction working width, not permanent easement" },
    { "id": "joint_bay_interval_m",
      "value": [500, 1000], "unit": "m",
      "source": "national-grid-undergrounding-technical-issues" },
    { "id": "watercourse_span_large_m",
      "value": 150, "unit": "m",
      "source": "iet-parsons-brinckerhoff-2012" },
    { "id": "trench_section_m",
      "value": [1.5, 1.2], "unit": "m",
      "note": "width, depth, per cable trench" }
  ]
}

Three things that buys you. The router reads penalties and widths from a product instead of hardcoding them. Every civil number on a card can cite a source the way the fault currents already cite a sheet and a row. And when a parameter is challenged, the answer is a document and a page rather than an argument.

What not to write: anything reading as design advice, sizing, or a route recommendation. The repository’s not_a_connection_assessment line is the boundary, and civil parameters sit on the same side of it — published typical values, never a proposal.

10 The corridor tracer — a second feature, not a replacement

The straight line stays exactly as it is. The tracer is additive, behind a deliberate action, and drawn only when asked for.

The gesture

The v8 engine binds click, dblclick, mousemove, mousedown, mouseup and keydown. It binds no contextmenu and no long-press — both slots are free, on desktop and on touch.

surfacegesturewhy it is safe
desktopright-click on the map or on a substation Nothing binds contextmenu today, so no existing behaviour is displaced.
touchlong-press, about 500 ms, with a movement threshold so a pan never fires it No touchstart handler exists; pan and pinch stay untouched.
botha ⟶ TRACE tile beside Zone Draw and the radius tool Follows the collapsible-panel pattern already in the engine, so it is discoverable without a gesture.

What it draws

A stream of neon corridors from the chosen point to the nearest connection points, coloured by the voltage class of the target, reusing the palette already carrying the 400 / 275 / 132 / 66 / 33 kV layers. Anyone who knows the map knows instantly that a blue corridor lands at 400 kV.

  • Animated dash offset flowing toward the substation — direction carried by motion, not an arrowhead
  • Each corridor labelled: routed km, straight km, factor, crossings by class
  • Corridors failing the swathe-width test drawn dashed and dimmed, with the reason on hover
  • Snap distance drawn as a distinct stub at each end, never silently absorbed into the route
  • Click a corridor to open the substation card that already exists
  • prefers-reduced-motion stops the flow and leaves the corridors static

Exploration mode — where this gets interesting

The card already names the sites a circuit reaches: CULHAM JET, DIDCOT, EAST CLAYDON, LEIGHTON BUZZARD, MINETY, WALHAM. Trace from a substation rather than from a project and those become traceable onward — a second press walks the published network one hop further, each circuit drawn in its own voltage colour. The result is a user pulling the grid graph outward by hand, which is the thing no other public tool lets anyone do.

The line this feature must not cross

A drawn corridor will be read as a route someone could build. It is a corridor length along public road geometry — not a wayleave, not a consent, not a design, not a cost. The label has to carry that as firmly as the panel already carries a rating is not what is free on the circuit. Drawing it beautifully raises that risk rather than lowering it.
